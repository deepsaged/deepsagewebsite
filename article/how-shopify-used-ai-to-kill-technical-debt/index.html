How Shopify Used AI to Kill Technical Debt

While everyone else is using LLMs to write more messy code, Shopify is using them to clean up the mess.

deepsage.com/s/bVl0eQ

The Spaghetti Monster is real

Imagine walking into a kitchen where every single drawer is filled with a random assortment of forks, loose screws, and dried pasta. That is what a massive, aging codebase looks like after a decade of rapid growth. For Shopify, this wasn't just a metaphor; it was a mounting pile of technical debt that threatened to slow their entire engineering engine to a crawl.

For years, the standard industry approach to scaling was to just keep adding more layers. More features, more microservices, more complexity. It’s the software equivalent of adding a new room to your house every week without ever bothering to check if the foundation is still solid. Eventually, you end up with a structure that looks less like a home and more like a structural engineering nightmare waiting to happen.

The AI paradox

Most people assume that the rise of generative AI is just an invitation to write even more garbage code, faster. The logic is simple: if an LLM can generate 50 lines of code in three seconds, why bother with the tedious work of refactoring or following strict architectural patterns? Why not just let the bots churn out a mountain of functional—but unreadable—spaghetti?

This is the 'slop' problem. If we use AI to increase the volume of code without increasing the quality of our standards, we aren't actually being more productive; we're just creating a larger pile of trash to clean up later. It’s like using a high-speed printing press to churn out instruction manuals written in a language no one understands.

The Quality Pivot

Shopify used AI to identify and refactor legacy code patterns rather than just generating new, unvetted features.

Turning the tide with automated refactoring

Shopify took a different path. Instead of using AI as a pure generation engine, they pivoted toward using it as a sophisticated cleanup crew. They leveraged AI to scan their massive repositories, identify patterns of messy or outdated code, and—this is the crucial part—suggest or implement cleaner, more standardized alternatives.

This wasn't about replacing engineers with robots (sorry, devs, your jobs are safe for now); it was about using automated code analysis to handle the heavy lifting of modernization. The process works like this:

  • Identification: The AI flags sections of code that deviate from the current, optimized standards.
  • Contextual Understanding: Unlike a simple 'find and replace' tool, the model understands how the change affects the rest of the system.
  • Refactoring: The AI proposes a version of the code that adheres to modern, cleaner patterns.
  • Validation: The system runs the new code through rigorous testing to ensure nothing broke in the process.

Why this matters for your workflow

This shift is a signal to the entire industry. We are moving away from the era of 'AI as a typewriter' and into the era of 'AI as a curator.' The real value isn't in the ability to generate new text, but in the ability to synthesize, organize, and improve existing information.

If you are a developer, this means your value is shifting from 'knowing the syntax' to 'knowing the architecture.' If you are a student, it means learning how to prompt for structure and quality is just as important as learning how to prompt for output. We are seeing the emergence of intelligent software maintenance as a core competency.

In a world where code is becoming cheaper to produce, the cost of maintaining that code is becoming the real bottleneck. Shopify's success suggests that the winners of the AI revolution won't be the ones who write the most code, but the ones who manage the complexity of what they've already written.

So, as you look at your own projects or your current workload, ask yourself: are you using AI to build a faster way to create chaos, or are you using it to build something that actually lasts?
