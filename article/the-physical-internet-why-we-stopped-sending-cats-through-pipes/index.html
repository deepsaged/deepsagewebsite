The Physical Internet: Why We Stopped Sending Cats Through Pipes

Before we had packets of data, we had packets of everything-including live cats and champagne.

deepsage.com/s/er3OA8

The Original Packet Switching

On October 7, 1897, New York City decided that the best way to handle the mail was to stop using streets and start using vacuum-sealed iron pipes. The goal was simple: move things faster by removing the friction of human error, traffic, and weather.

I once tried to solve the problem of morning traffic by building a pressurized steam-vent system under my driveway to launch my car directly onto the FDR Drive. It worked brilliantly for about four seconds, provided you didn't mind the car arriving at its destination as a fine metallic mist. The New York pneumatic tube system was much more stable. It used compressed air and vacuum suction to propel cylindrical canisters through a network of tubes buried four to twelve feet underground at speeds of up to 35 miles per hour.

At its peak, this system spanned several major cities, including Philadelphia and Boston. It was, essentially, the physical precursor to the internet. Instead of packets of data, we had packets of paper, heavy goods, and, occasionally, highly perishable items.

The Payload Problem

In the digital age, we don't worry about the 'payload' of an email being too heavy. If you send a 50GB file, the network just takes a little longer. In the pneumatic era, the payload was literal.

The system was remarkably versatile. On any given Tuesday, a canister might contain a stack of newspapers, a collection of legal documents, or-as documented in the official records-a dozen eggs. The 'payload' could even include a full bottle of champagne or, quite famously, a live cat.

When the system was inaugurated, the first test payloads were quite dignified: a Bible and the works of a famous statesman. But the beauty of a physical network is its lack of protocol constraints. If the canister can fit, the data can flow. This versatility was its greatest strength, but also its undoing. The system was incredibly robust for 'small' data-letters and light parcels-but it struggled with the 'bandwidth' of larger, heavier objects. You can't exactly route a crate of heavy machinery through a pneumatic tube designed for a ham sandwich.

The Scalability Crisis

This is where we encounter the fundamental flaw that eventually killed the system: the lack of scalability.

In modern networking, if we need more bandwidth, we upgrade the fiber optics. In the pneumatic network, if you wanted to send something larger, you had to physically rebuild the infrastructure. There was no 'software update' for a four-inch iron pipe.

As the volume of commerce grew, the physical limitations of the tubes became a bottleneck. The system was highly efficient for high-frequency, low-mass' communication-the 19th-century equivalent of an instant message. However, as the 'data' (goods) became heavier and more complex, the energy required to move that mass through fixed-diameter pipes became economically unsustainable.

By the time the mid-20th century arrived, the cost of maintaining the physical 'hardware'-the iron pipes, the massive air pumps, and the specialized stations-outweighed the utility of the service. The system couldn't scale up to meet the demands of a modern, heavy-goods economy. We didn't just need faster pipes; we needed bigger ones, and the city streets simply couldn't accommodate a massive, city-wide expansion of pneumatic diameters.

The Legacy of the Tube

Eventually, the pipes were decommissioned, replaced by the much more scalable (though much less exciting) internal combustion engine and the digital bit. We traded the thrill of watching a canister arrive with a surprise payload for the efficiency of the logistics industry.

Today, we live in the world of the digital packet, where 'weight' is an abstraction. We no longer fear a 'heavy' email breaking our network, because our network is made of light. But there is a certain nostalgia for the era of the pneumatic tube-a time when the network was something you could touch, something that could carry a cat, and something that, for a brief moment in history, made the world feel much smaller and much more connected, one pressurized canister at a time.
