The Physical Internet: Why We Stopped Sending Cats Through Pipes

The Original Packet Switching

On October 7, 1897, New York City decided that the best way to handle the mail was to stop using streets and start using vacuum-sealed iron pipes. The goal was simple: move things faster by removing the friction of human error, traffic, and weather.

I once tried to solve the problem of morning traffic by building a pressurized steam-vent system under my driveway to launch my car directly onto the FDR Drive. It worked brilliantly for about four seconds, provided you didn't mind the car arriving at its destination as a fine metallic mist. The New York pneumatic tube system was much more stable. It used compressed air and vacuum suction to propel cylindrical canisters through a network of tubes buried a few feet beneath the streets, at speeds of up to 35 miles per hour.

New York wasn't alone: similar systems ran in other U.S. cities, including Philadelphia (the first, in 1893), Boston, Chicago and St. Louis. New York's own network eventually grew to about 27 miles of tubes linking 23 post offices. It was, essentially, the physical precursor to the internet. Instead of packets of data, it moved canisters of letters, newspapers and the occasional oddity.

The Payload Problem

In the digital age, we don't worry about the 'payload' of an email being too heavy. If you send a 50GB file, the network just takes a little longer. In the pneumatic era, the payload was literal.

The system was remarkably versatile. Mostly, the canisters carried letters, around 600 at a time. But the tubes also became famous for stranger cargo: accounts from the period mention clothing, a candlestick and, most famously, a live cat, which reportedly emerged dazed and then ran off before it was caught.

When the system was inaugurated, the first payload was suitably dignified: a Bible wrapped in an American flag, a copy of the Constitution and President McKinley's inaugural address. But the beauty of a physical network is its lack of protocol constraints. If the canister can fit, the data can flow. The catch was size. The tubes were great for 'small' data like letters, but nothing bigger than a canister about eight inches across could squeeze through. You can't exactly route a crate of heavy machinery through a pneumatic tube designed for a ham sandwich.

The Scalability Crisis

This is where we encounter the flaws that eventually killed the system: cost and a lack of scalability.

In modern networking, if we need more bandwidth, we upgrade the fiber optics. In the pneumatic network, if you wanted to send something larger, you had to physically rebuild the infrastructure. There was no 'software update' for an iron pipe sized for eight-inch canisters.

The system was highly efficient for 'high-frequency, low-mass' communication: the 19th-century equivalent of an instant message. But it was expensive. The Post Office paid rent of about $17,000 per mile per year for the tubes, and a breakdown could mean digging up the street to find a stuck canister. Mail trucks, meanwhile, kept getting cheaper and more capable.

Service was suspended during World War I, restored in 1922 after lobbying by the tube contractors, and finally halted in 1953, when the Post Office decided the physical 'hardware' (the iron pipes, the massive air pumps and the specialized stations) was no longer worth the cost. The tubes couldn't compete with trucks that carried far more mail per trip, and nobody was going to dig up Manhattan to lay bigger pipes.

The Legacy of the Tube

Eventually, the pipes were decommissioned, replaced by the much more scalable (though much less exciting) internal combustion engine and the digital bit. We traded the thrill of watching a canister arrive with a surprise payload for the efficiency of the logistics industry.

Today, we live in the world of the digital packet, where 'weight' is an abstraction. We no longer fear a 'heavy' email breaking our network, because our network is made of light. But there is a certain nostalgia for the era of the pneumatic tube-a time when the network was something you could touch, something that could carry a cat, and something that, for a brief moment in history, made the world feel much smaller and much more connected, one pressurized canister at a time.
