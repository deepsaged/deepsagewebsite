The Screwdriver That Triggered a Nuclear Nightmare

Key Takeaways
  • The Demon Core caused two fatal radiation accidents in 1945 and 1946.
  • Accidental criticality occurred when neutron reflectors were improperly positioned.
  • The core's plutonium was eventually melted down and recycled in 1946.

A blue light flashed. A wave of intense heat hit the room. Then, a security guard named Patrick Cleary did the only sensible thing: he ran. It was 3:20 PM on a Tuesday in May 1946, and inside a small lab in Los Alamos, the laws of physics had just decided to become very personal and very aggressive.

This wasn't a sci-fi movie; it was a Tuesday afternoon in the middle of what scientists called 'Tickling the Tail of the Dragon.' The 'dragon' in question was the Demon Core, a 6.2-kilogram sphere of plutonium-gallium alloy. It was roughly the size of a softball, but it possessed the potential to turn a room into a microwave oven if you weren't careful.

The 'Dragon' and its bad habit

The Demon Core wasn't some legendary monster from a fantasy novel; it was a piece of hardware intended for a third nuclear weapon in the Pacific Theater. When Japan surrendered, the core wasn't sent to the front lines. Instead, it stayed in New Mexico to be used for criticality experiments, which is a fancy way of saying scientists were trying to see exactly how much 'extra' material they could add before things went boom.

The Core's Purpose

The Demon Core was originally intended for a third nuclear weapon to be dropped on Japan.

Criticality is a delicate balance. You want enough neutrons to keep a chain reaction going, but not so many that the whole thing becomes a 'prompt critical event'-a sudden, uncontrolled burst of radiation. To test this, scientists used neutron-reflective tampers, like tungsten carbide bricks or beryllium shells, to bounce neutrons back into the core. It's essentially playing a high-stakes game of 'how close can I get to the edge without falling in?'

Two accidents, one hospital room

If you think the first accident was bad, the second one was essentially a masterclass in what not to do during a lab demo. In August 1945, Harry Daghlian was working late, alone, trying to build a neutron reflector. He was adding a tungsten carbide brick when he accidentally dropped it onto the core. The mass went supercritical. He died 25 days later.

Fast forward to May 1946. Enter Louis Slotin. Slotin was a heavy hitter-he had actually assembled the core for the Trinity test. For his experiment, he was using two beryllium-coated hemispheres. The goal was to bring them together without them actually touching. He was using a screwdriver to keep the gap open.

The Fatal Slip

A single slipping screwdriver caused the beryllium hemispheres to crash, triggering a lethal radiation burst.

It's the ultimate 'oops' moment in scientific history. The screwdriver slipped. The hemispheres crashed together. The core went supercritical, releasing a lethal dose of radiation. Slotin, in a moment of pure, unadulterated grit, used his bare hands to scatter the hemispheres and stop the reaction. He saved the others in the room, but he paid the ultimate price. In a grimly poetic twist, both Daghliente and Slotin eventually died in the same hospital room at the U.S. Engineers Hospital.

The math of a tragedy

How do you even begin to measure a disaster like this? You don't look at a Geiger counter after the fact-you draw a map. After the second accident, the team literally drew a diagram to estimate who got hit. They mapped out the distances: Slotin was just 1.5 feet from the source; others were 3, 6, or even 16 feet away.

radiation doses were estimated not from broken badges (which were conveniently 100 feet away in a lead box), but from the levels of sodium found in the men's blood. It’s a level of forensic math that sounds more like a crime scene investigation than a laboratory report.

Why we still talk about the 'Demon'

At the time, the Manhattan Project was a whirlwind of high-pressure innovation and, frankly, some pretty questionable safety protocols. While the Demon Core gets the most fame, it was part of a larger, darker tally of 24 deaths at Los Alamos during the project-ranging from construction accidents and car crashes to a sudden, accidental smoke bomb explosion.

Eventually, the Demon Core was melted down in the summer of 1946, and its plutonium was recycled. The 'demon' was gone, but the lesson remained: when you're playing with the fundamental forces of the universe, a slipping screwdriver isn't just a nuisance-it's a catastrophe.

Frequently Asked

It was a 6.2-kilogram sphere made of a plutonium-gallium alloy.

Did the scientists survive the accidents?

Harry Daghlian and Louis Slotin both died from acute radiation syndrome following the two separate accidents.
